A starting register of the roles where insider risk is most consequential: people whose access, trust and opportunity mean a bad day, a bad decision or a bad actor would do the most damage. Each role says why it carries more risk, what access usually comes with it, which indicators matter more there, and which controls fit, with the public sources behind it. Use it to start your own register, not to replace one.

Role is one factor, not a judgment about people. Most people in every role below are the reason the organization works. A role lands on this list because of what it can reach and change, not because of who holds it. Designating a role high-risk should change the controls around the role: screening, privileges, separation of duties, monitoring scope and offboarding. It should not, by itself, be a reason for suspicion of or adverse action against any person. Tell people which roles are designated and why, keep monitoring proportionate, and bring legal, privacy and HR in before you start.

How to read this register

  • No public source gives a cross-sector list. The research behind this page looked for one in U.S. federal policy, financial regulators, CERT, NIST, ISO, PCI, SOC 2 and UK NPSA guidance. What exists are methods for designating positions (OPM's risk and sensitivity designations, NIST PS-2, NPSA's role-based assessment) and a few short regulator lists, the clearest being New York DFS's three categories of sensitive positions. This register is a synthesis of those sources plus practitioner experience; each role cites what supports it.
  • Four drivers. Every role is described by access (what it can reach), trust (how much it is allowed to do without checks), opportunity (what misuse would look like and how easy it is to hide) and pressure (what pushes people in this role, or makes outsiders target them). Pressure describes the situation around the role, never a trait of the person in it.
  • Indicators are role-specific observables, things that mean more in this role than elsewhere. They are prompts for review, not evidence of wrongdoing; most have innocent explanations.
  • Controls are starting points. Scale them to your organization, your law and your risk register. Monitoring scope in particular must follow your jurisdiction's privacy and employment law.
  • Sectors. Most roles apply anywhere. Financial institution and healthcare roles are tagged; use the sector filter to show only those.

Technology and privileged access 5 roles

People who can change the systems everyone else depends on, including the logs and controls that would show what they did.

System and network administrators Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Administrative rights on servers, endpoints, network gear and backups, often across the whole estate.
Trust
Trusted to act without per-action approval, frequently out of hours.
Opportunity
Can create accounts, change configurations, disable logging and touch backups; sabotage and data theft both fit inside normal-looking work.
Pressure
On-call load, layoffs and outsourcing hit this group; grievances about being undervalued are a recurring theme in IT sabotage cases.

Typical access and privileges

  • Domain or root administrator
  • Hypervisor and storage consoles
  • Backup systems
  • Firewall, VPN and remote-access management
  • Log and monitoring pipelines

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Privileged sessions with no matching change ticket
  • New accounts, keys or scheduled tasks created outside the change process
  • Logging, EDR or backup jobs disabled or reconfigured
  • Unapproved remote-access tools or tunnels
  • Access to systems outside the person's assigned scope

Recommended controls

Screening
Elevated pre-hire screening proportionate to the role, rescreening on promotion into wider admin scope.
Privileged access
Separate admin accounts, just-in-time elevation, vaulted credentials, no standing domain-wide rights.
Separation of duties
No single admin can both change and approve production changes, or both administer and erase the logs that record them.
Monitoring scope
Session recording or command logging for privileged sessions, shipped to a log store the admin cannot alter.
Offboarding
Same-day revocation of every privileged credential, key and token; rotate shared secrets the person knew.

Sources: [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, [10] New York State Department of Financial Services, [5] U.S. Department of Defense, [6] Committee on National Security Systems, [16] ISO/IEC

Cloud, SaaS and identity administrators Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Tenant-wide control of cloud accounts, identity providers, email and collaboration platforms.
Trust
Can grant themselves or others access to almost anything, usually through a web console from anywhere.
Opportunity
One role assignment, mailbox delegation or API token can expose an entire tenant; actions can look like routine administration.
Pressure
Same pressures as other administrators, plus a high outside market value that makes them recruitment targets.

Typical access and privileges

  • Identity provider global or privileged role administrator
  • Cloud organization owner or root account
  • Email and collaboration tenant admin
  • SaaS super-admin roles
  • API keys and service principals

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Self-assigned or temporary role grants that are not removed
  • Mail forwarding rules, delegation or eDiscovery searches without a case
  • New OAuth apps, service principals or long-lived keys
  • Conditional-access or MFA policy exceptions
  • Console sign-ins from unusual locations or devices

Recommended controls

Screening
As for system administrators.
Privileged access
Privileged identity management with time-bound, approved activation; break-glass accounts sealed and alerted on.
Separation of duties
Role assignment and policy changes for privileged roles require a second approver.
Monitoring scope
Tenant audit logs exported to a store outside the admins' control; alert on privileged role changes and mailbox access by admins.
Offboarding
Remove role assignments, revoke sessions and refresh tokens, and inventory keys and apps the person created.

Sources: [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, [16] ISO/IEC, [17] AICPA & CIMA

Developers and DevOps engineers with production access Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Source code, build pipelines, deployment rights, secrets and sometimes production data.
Trust
Code they write runs with the organization's authority, and review is often lighter than assumed.
Opportunity
Logic bombs, backdoors, data exfiltration through code or pipelines, and theft of source code as intellectual property.
Pressure
Departure to competitors, disputes over ownership of work, and layoffs.

Typical access and privileges

  • Source repositories
  • CI/CD pipelines and deployment credentials
  • Secrets managers
  • Production shells or debugging access
  • Copies of production data in lower environments

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Commits that bypass review or land outside normal hours just before departure
  • Pipeline or branch-protection changes
  • Bulk cloning of repositories outside the person's projects
  • Secrets read that the person's services do not use
  • Production data copied to personal or unmanaged locations

Recommended controls

Screening
Standard screening; elevated for engineers with standing production or secrets access.
Privileged access
No standing production access; time-bound break-glass with approval and recording.
Separation of duties
Mandatory peer review and protected branches so no one ships alone; deploy rights separate from approve rights.
Monitoring scope
Repository, pipeline and secrets-access audit logs; watch for bulk clone and archive activity.
Offboarding
Revoke Git, cloud and pipeline credentials; rotate secrets the person could read; review their recent commits.

Sources: [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [20] MITRE Center for Threat-Informed Defense, [16] ISO/IEC

Database administrators and data engineers Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Direct access to the data itself, below the application controls that normally limit what a user sees.
Trust
Expected to query, copy and move large datasets as part of the job.
Opportunity
Bulk extraction of customer, financial or health records, and quiet alteration of records.
Pressure
Data has a ready resale market; this role is an obvious target for outside recruitment.

Typical access and privileges

  • Database owner or sysadmin roles
  • Data warehouse and lake administration
  • ETL and replication jobs
  • Backups and exports

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Queries pulling full tables of sensitive fields with no matching request
  • New exports, replication targets or scheduled jobs to unfamiliar destinations
  • Direct edits to records that normally change only through applications
  • Database audit settings changed

Recommended controls

Screening
Elevated screening where the data includes financial, health or other regulated records.
Privileged access
Named accounts, not shared ones; just-in-time elevation to owner roles.
Separation of duties
Changes to production data through approved change paths, not ad hoc DML.
Monitoring scope
Database activity monitoring on sensitive tables, shipped off the database server.
Offboarding
Revoke database and pipeline access; review recent exports and jobs.

Sources: [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [14] U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, [18] PCI Security Standards Council

Service accounts, automation and AI agents acting for someone Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Often holds broader and longer-lived access than any person, because it was set up once and never revisited.
Trust
Its actions are attributed to a system, not a person, so they draw less scrutiny.
Opportunity
A person who controls the account or the agent's instructions can act through it; an agent can also act wrongly on its own.
Pressure
Not applicable to the account; applies to whoever controls it.

Typical access and privileges

  • Service principals and API keys
  • Automation and RPA bots
  • AI agents with tool or data access
  • Scheduled jobs with stored credentials

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Interactive sign-in by an account that should only run unattended
  • Agent or bot actions outside its defined task
  • Credential or prompt changes by someone who does not own the account

Recommended controls

Screening
Not applicable; screen and record the human owner.
Privileged access
Every non-human identity has a named human owner, least privilege, and an expiry.
Separation of duties
The person who builds an agent or bot does not alone approve its permissions.
Monitoring scope
Log agent and service actions with the owning human recorded; see A-AGENT01 in the framework.
Offboarding
When an owner leaves, reassign or disable their service accounts and agents the same day.

Sources: [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [1] Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency

Security and oversight functions 2 roles

The people who watch everyone else. They need the same controls, applied by someone outside their own chain.

SOC, insider risk and investigations analysts Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Monitoring data about everyone: user activity, email, HR case notes, investigation files.
Trust
Trusted to look at sensitive information about colleagues and to keep it confidential.
Opportunity
Curiosity lookups, leaking an investigation, tipping off a subject, or tuning detections to blind the tooling.
Pressure
Exposure to distressing material, workload and moral injury; see the Wellness page.

Typical access and privileges

  • SIEM and UAM consoles
  • DLP and email review tools
  • Case management systems
  • HR and badge data feeds
  • Detection rule and allow-list configuration

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Searches on colleagues with no open case or tasking
  • Exports of case material
  • Detection rules, exclusions or allow-lists changed without review
  • Alerts about the analyst's own activity closed by the analyst

Recommended controls

Screening
Elevated screening and periodic rescreening, as for other privileged roles.
Privileged access
Access tied to assigned cases; no standing access to every subject's data.
Separation of duties
No analyst reviews alerts on themselves or their own team; detection changes reviewed by a second person.
Monitoring scope
Audit the auditors: a separate function reviews analyst queries and case access.
Offboarding
Immediate removal from tools and cases; hand off open investigations under supervision.

Sources: [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [6] Committee on National Security Systems

Legal, compliance, internal audit and ethics staff Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Privileged legal material, regulatory correspondence, audit findings, whistleblower reports, M&A plans.
Trust
Often have broad read access by design so they can test or advise anywhere.
Opportunity
Leaking privileged or market-moving information, suppressing findings, or trading on what they know.
Pressure
Conflicts between loyalty and duty, and outside interest in what they hold.

Typical access and privileges

  • Matter and contract management systems
  • Audit work papers
  • Hotline and ethics case data
  • Board and regulator correspondence

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Access to matters outside the person's assignments
  • Downloads of deal or regulatory material near personal trading windows
  • Findings or hotline cases closed without the normal review

Recommended controls

Screening
Standard screening; elevated for staff on market-sensitive deals.
Privileged access
Matter-based access and ethical walls rather than blanket read rights.
Separation of duties
Findings and hotline closures need a second reviewer; auditors do not audit work they performed.
Monitoring scope
Monitor access to deal rooms and hotline data; pair with personal trading pre-clearance where relevant.
Offboarding
Revoke matter access and remind of continuing confidentiality obligations.

Sources: [3] U.S. Office of Personnel Management, [16] ISO/IEC, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute

Money movement and finance 2 roles

Roles that can move, record or approve money. Regulators have treated these as sensitive positions for decades.

Wire, payments and treasury staff Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Can initiate, approve or release funds transfers and change beneficiary details.
Trust
Trusted with large sums and urgent requests.
Opportunity
Fraudulent transfers, beneficiary changes, or collusion with outside fraudsters, including business email compromise.
Pressure
Personal financial strain, gambling, or coercion by outside criminals.

Typical access and privileges

  • Wire and ACH origination systems
  • Treasury management portals
  • Payment approval queues
  • Beneficiary and vendor bank detail records

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Transfers just under approval thresholds
  • Beneficiary changes followed quickly by payments
  • Overrides of callback or verification steps
  • Reluctance to take leave or let others cover the desk

Recommended controls

Screening
Elevated screening including credit history where lawful; rescreen on promotion to approver.
Privileged access
Payment entitlements by role, reviewed at least quarterly.
Separation of duties
Dual control: one person initiates, another approves, and beneficiary changes are verified out of band.
Monitoring scope
Payment anomaly monitoring and review of overrides; mandatory time away with no system access.
Offboarding
Remove payment entitlements and tokens before notice is announced where practical.

Sources: [11] Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, [10] New York State Department of Financial Services, [3] U.S. Office of Personnel Management, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute

Accounts payable, receivable and general ledger staff Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Vendor master data, invoices, journal entries and reconciliations.
Trust
Trusted to keep the books accurate with limited line-by-line review.
Opportunity
Fictitious vendors, invoice and refund schemes, and journal entries that hide losses.
Pressure
Personal financial pressure; pressure to hit targets.

Typical access and privileges

  • ERP accounts payable and receivable modules
  • Vendor master file
  • Journal entry and close processes
  • Bank reconciliation

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Vendors created and paid by the same person
  • Vendor bank details matching employee details
  • Manual journal entries near period end without support
  • Reconciliations always done by one person who resists help

Recommended controls

Screening
Standard screening; elevated where the role can both create and pay vendors.
Privileged access
Restrict vendor master changes to a small group outside AP.
Separation of duties
Separate vendor setup, invoice entry, payment approval and reconciliation.
Monitoring scope
Vendor-employee data matching and journal entry analytics.
Offboarding
Revoke ERP roles and review the person's recent vendor and journal activity.

Sources: [10] New York State Department of Financial Services, [11] Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, [16] ISO/IEC, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute

People data 1 role

Roles that hold information about everyone else, including the information an insider risk program depends on.

HR, payroll and benefits staff Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Personal data on the whole workforce: identity documents, salaries, bank details, medical and disciplinary records.
Trust
Trusted with confidential employee matters, including upcoming terminations and investigations.
Opportunity
Ghost employees, pay changes, identity theft, leaking terminations or tipping off a subject.
Pressure
Exposure to conflict and difficult cases; personal relationships with affected staff.

Typical access and privileges

  • HRIS and payroll systems
  • Employee relations case files
  • Benefits and leave records
  • Background check results

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Pay or bank detail changes made and approved by the same person
  • Records viewed for employees outside the person's client group
  • Employees added without a matching requisition
  • Early knowledge of terminations showing up elsewhere

Recommended controls

Screening
Elevated screening for payroll and employee relations roles.
Privileged access
Field-level access so most HR staff cannot see bank details or medical records.
Separation of duties
Payroll changes entered by one person and approved by another; new hires reconciled to requisitions.
Monitoring scope
Audit access to sensitive HR records and payroll changes.
Offboarding
Revoke HRIS access promptly; HR staff are also the people who start everyone else's offboarding, so plan who starts theirs.

Sources: [3] U.S. Office of Personnel Management, [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [16] ISO/IEC, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute

Leadership and the people around them 2 roles

Authority, not technical access, is what makes these roles high consequence.

Executives and board members Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Strategy, M&A, financial results, and authority to approve exceptions.
Trust
Rarely subject to the controls they approve for others.
Opportunity
Overriding controls, insider trading, leaking strategy, or directing others to act improperly.
Pressure
High stakes, public scrutiny, and targeting by foreign intelligence, activists and criminals.

Typical access and privileges

  • Board materials
  • Deal rooms
  • Exception and override authority
  • Delegated access through assistants

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Requests to bypass normal approvals for urgency
  • Exceptions granted to themselves or close associates
  • Unusual access to deal or results information outside their remit

Recommended controls

Screening
Elevated screening at appointment, proportionate to role and law.
Privileged access
Executives do not hold standing administrator rights.
Separation of duties
Exceptions and overrides by executives are logged and reviewed by the board or audit committee.
Monitoring scope
Governance oversight rather than routine activity monitoring; protect them as targets too.
Offboarding
Plan departures in advance, including board materials, devices and delegated access.

Sources: [3] U.S. Office of Personnel Management, [10] New York State Department of Financial Services, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute

Executive assistants and chiefs of staff Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Delegated access to an executive's email, calendar, files and sometimes approval authority.
Trust
Act on the executive's behalf every day; their actions look like the executive's.
Opportunity
Leaking calendars and board material, approving in the executive's name, or being the route a social engineer uses.
Pressure
Long hours, proximity to power without its rewards, and direct targeting by outsiders.

Typical access and privileges

  • Mailbox and calendar delegation
  • Executive document stores
  • Expense and travel approval
  • Board portal administration

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Delegated mailbox access used outside working patterns
  • Forwarding or sharing executive material externally
  • Approvals in the executive's name the executive does not recognize

Recommended controls

Screening
Screen to the level of the executive they support.
Privileged access
Scope delegation to what the job needs and review it when either person changes role.
Separation of duties
Assistants do not approve their own expenses or changes to their own access.
Monitoring scope
Audit delegated access to executive mailboxes and files.
Offboarding
Remove delegations the same day; tell the executive.

Sources: [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute

Procurement and vendor management 1 role

Roles that decide who gets paid and who gets let in.

Procurement and vendor management staff Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Contract award, vendor onboarding, and often the decision to give a supplier access to systems or sites.
Trust
Trusted to choose suppliers fairly.
Opportunity
Kickbacks, bid rigging, conflicts of interest, and onboarding a supplier that then becomes an access path.
Pressure
Outside inducements from suppliers.

Typical access and privileges

  • Sourcing and contract systems
  • Vendor onboarding workflow
  • Supplier risk assessments

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Repeated awards to the same supplier without competition
  • Undisclosed relationships with suppliers
  • Supplier access approved without the normal risk review

Recommended controls

Screening
Standard screening plus annual conflict-of-interest declarations.
Privileged access
Separate the authority to award from the authority to grant supplier system access.
Separation of duties
Requisition, award, receipt and payment handled by different people.
Monitoring scope
Spend analytics and conflict-of-interest data matching.
Offboarding
Hand over open sourcing events under supervision.

Sources: [16] ISO/IEC, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology

Physical security and facilities 1 role

Roles that control who and what gets into buildings, and can move unescorted where others cannot.

Physical security, facilities and maintenance staff Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Badge systems, keys, cameras, plant rooms, data centers and after-hours access.
Trust
Trusted to be anywhere, at any time, often unescorted.
Opportunity
Theft, sabotage, letting others in, or deleting camera footage.
Pressure
Often lower-paid and contracted out, with less connection to the organization.

Typical access and privileges

  • Physical access control administration
  • Master keys
  • CCTV systems
  • Building management and plant systems
  • Data center floors

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Badge rights granted outside the request process
  • Camera gaps or deleted footage
  • After-hours presence in areas unrelated to work orders
  • Tailgating or propping doors for others

Recommended controls

Screening
Elevated screening for access-control administrators and staff with data center or vault access.
Privileged access
Badge administration separate from badge request approval; key control logs.
Separation of duties
No one both grants and audits physical access.
Monitoring scope
Correlate badge, camera and work-order data; review access-control admin changes.
Offboarding
Recover badges and keys and re-key where needed; remove access-control admin rights.

Sources: [1] Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, [9] National Protective Security Authority (NPSA), United Kingdom, [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology

Contractors and third parties 1 role

Insiders by access, outsiders by employer. NPSA lists facilitation of third-party access as one of its five insider risk types.

Contractors, consultants, MSPs and outsourced staff with standing access Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Often the same system and site access as employees, sometimes more, through managed-service tooling.
Trust
Trusted under a contract, but screened and supervised by someone else.
Opportunity
Access that outlives the engagement, weak supervision, and shared accounts.
Pressure
Short tenures, lower loyalty, and pressures the hiring organization cannot see.

Typical access and privileges

  • Named or shared accounts
  • Remote management tools from the provider
  • Badges
  • Copies of data in the provider's environment

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Access continuing after the engagement end date
  • Shared or generic accounts in use
  • Provider remote tools connecting outside agreed windows

Recommended controls

Screening
Contract requires screening equivalent to employees in comparable roles, with evidence.
Privileged access
Named accounts, time-bound, with an internal sponsor who reviews them.
Separation of duties
Contractors do not approve their own access or work.
Monitoring scope
Same monitoring scope as employees in the equivalent role, stated in the contract.
Offboarding
Provider must notify transfers and terminations within an agreed time; sponsor confirms removal.

Sources: [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, [12] Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council, [16] ISO/IEC, [9] National Protective Security Authority (NPSA), United Kingdom

Transient high-risk states 2 roles

Not roles but moments. Anyone can pass through them, and the risk attaches to the moment, not the person.

Departing employees (resignation, notice period, termination, layoff) Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Still holds full access while their attachment to the organization ends.
Trust
Trust is often unchanged in systems even when the relationship has changed.
Opportunity
Taking data, customer lists or code to the next job; sabotage after an adverse separation.
Pressure
Grievance, financial worry, or a new employer's expectations.

Typical access and privileges

  • Whatever the person held in their role

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Bulk downloads, printing or uploads to personal storage after notice
  • Access to material unrelated to handover
  • Forwarding work email to personal accounts
  • Deleting files or changing passwords on shared resources

Recommended controls

Screening
Not applicable.
Privileged access
Reduce privileges at notice; remove admin rights before an adverse separation is announced.
Separation of duties
Handover is supervised by the manager, not done alone.
Monitoring scope
A defined, disclosed look-back and look-forward window around departure, with HR notifying security at notice.
Offboarding
Same-day revocation, asset recovery, exit interview covering continuing obligations, and retained access to the person's data for the organization.

Sources: [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [1] Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, [16] ISO/IEC

People changing roles or teams Cross-sector

Why it is high-risk

Access
Old access is often kept while new access is added.
Trust
Accumulated access is rarely questioned.
Opportunity
Combinations of access that no single role should hold, breaking separation of duties.
Pressure
Usually none; this is a control gap more than a behavior risk.

Typical access and privileges

  • Union of every role the person has held

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Entitlements from a previous role still in use
  • Access combinations that break separation of duties

Recommended controls

Screening
Rescreen if the new role carries a higher designation.
Privileged access
Review and remove prior-role access at transfer.
Separation of duties
Check the new combination against separation-of-duties rules.
Monitoring scope
Access recertification by the new manager.
Offboarding
Not applicable.

Sources: [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology, [16] ISO/IEC

Financial institutions 4 roles

Banks and credit unions add roles with direct access to member or customer money and data. Section 205(d) and its bank equivalent already bar some people from these institutions entirely.

Tellers, branch staff and vault custodians Financial institutions

Why it is high-risk

Access
Cash, negotiable instruments and account maintenance at the point of service.
Trust
Trusted with cash every day and with members' identity documents.
Opportunity
Cash theft, unauthorized withdrawals from dormant or elderly members' accounts, and helping outsiders open fraudulent accounts.
Pressure
Lower pay, personal financial strain, and recruitment by organized fraud rings.

Typical access and privileges

  • Teller drawers and vault
  • Core banking account maintenance
  • Address and contact detail changes
  • New account opening

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Cash differences that recur
  • Transactions on dormant accounts
  • Address or phone changes followed by withdrawals
  • Overrides requested from the same supervisor repeatedly

Recommended controls

Screening
Section 205(d) or Section 19 checks as required, plus standard screening.
Privileged access
Override and maintenance rights limited and logged.
Separation of duties
Dual control on vaults and high-value transactions.
Monitoring scope
Teller differences, override and dormant-account monitoring; mandatory time away.
Offboarding
Balance and recover cash and keys; revoke core access.

Sources: [13] National Credit Union Administration, [11] Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, [10] New York State Department of Financial Services

Loan officers, underwriters and loan servicing Financial institutions

Why it is high-risk

Access
Credit decisions, collateral records and loan modifications.
Trust
Trusted to apply credit policy and to waive or modify terms.
Opportunity
Fraudulent loans to associates or straw borrowers, kickbacks, and hiding delinquencies.
Pressure
Production incentives and relationships with borrowers or brokers.

Typical access and privileges

  • Loan origination system
  • Underwriting exceptions
  • Servicing and modification tools

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Loans to related parties without disclosure
  • Repeated policy exceptions for the same broker or borrower group
  • Modifications or extensions that hide delinquency

Recommended controls

Screening
Section 205(d) or Section 19 checks plus elevated screening for lenders with approval authority.
Privileged access
Lending limits by role; exceptions above limits require a second approver.
Separation of duties
Origination, underwriting approval and funding by different people.
Monitoring scope
Related-party and exception reporting; loan review sampling.
Offboarding
Review the officer's recent approvals and exceptions.

Sources: [13] National Credit Union Administration, [10] New York State Department of Financial Services, [3] U.S. Office of Personnel Management

Fraud operations and BSA/AML analysts Financial institutions

Why it is high-risk

Access
Fraud and AML alerts, investigation files, and in some cases authority to release holds or close alerts.
Trust
Trusted to recognize and stop financial crime.
Opportunity
Clearing alerts for collaborators, tipping off subjects, or leaking SAR-related information.
Pressure
High alert volumes and targeting by the criminals they work against.

Typical access and privileges

  • Fraud and AML case management
  • Transaction monitoring rules and thresholds
  • Hold and release authority

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Alerts on connected parties closed by the same analyst
  • Threshold or rule changes without review
  • Case lookups with no assigned alert

Recommended controls

Screening
Elevated screening; Section 205(d) or Section 19 checks.
Privileged access
Rule and threshold changes restricted and logged.
Separation of duties
Quality review of closed alerts by someone else; no one works alerts involving people they know.
Monitoring scope
Audit case access and alert dispositions.
Offboarding
Reassign open cases under supervision.

Sources: [13] National Credit Union Administration, [8] Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, [2] National Institute of Standards and Technology

Contact center agents with account access Financial institutions

Why it is high-risk

Access
Look up and change member or customer accounts, often in high volume and remotely.
Trust
Trusted to verify callers and act on their behalf.
Opportunity
Selling account data, enabling account takeover by changing contact details or resetting credentials, and SIM-swap-style collusion.
Pressure
Lower pay, high turnover, and active recruitment by fraud groups, including through social media.

Typical access and privileges

  • Customer account lookup
  • Contact detail and credential reset
  • Card controls and limits

Indicators that matter more in this role

  • Lookups on accounts with no matching inbound contact
  • Contact or credential changes followed by fraud
  • Screen capture or photos of screens in remote settings

Recommended controls

Screening
Section 205(d) or Section 19 checks plus standard screening.
Privileged access
Account access tied to an active contact; high-risk changes require step-up verification.
Separation of duties
High-risk changes reviewed or confirmed out of band.
Monitoring scope
Lookup-without-contact analytics and linkage to downstream fraud.
Offboarding
Revoke access at end of shift on notice; collect remote equipment.

How to build your own high-risk roles register

The method below follows the shape of NPSA's role-based security risk assessment and NIST PS-2: start from what you must protect, work out which roles could do it the most harm, and review on a schedule. It is a triage aid for a program team. It is not a validated instrument, and the tiers it produces are not scores about people.

  1. Start from critical assets, not job titles. List the assets whose loss, exposure or corruption would hurt most: money movement, customer or member data, crown-jewel systems, safety systems, intellectual property, the security tooling itself. CERT's first practice and NPSA's first step both start here.
  2. Map roles to assets. For each asset, list the roles (positions, not named people) that can reach, change, approve or conceal changes to it. Include contractors, service accounts and AI agents that act for someone.
  3. Ask the same questions of every role.
    • Can this role reach a critical asset directly, below the application controls?
    • Can it act alone, or approve its own work?
    • Can it hide or erase evidence of what it did, including logs, footage or records?
    • How much damage could one person in this role do, and how fast?
    • Does it move money, change records of value, or hold data on many people?
    • Is it a known target for outside recruitment, coercion or social engineering?
    • Does anyone independent review its work today?
  4. Sort roles into a few tiers. Three is enough, for example standard, elevated and high. A practical rule: a role that can reach a critical asset and act alone or hide its actions is high; a role with one of those properties is elevated. Write the rule down so it is applied the same way every time, and record the reasons for each designation.
  5. Attach controls to tiers, not to people. For each tier, decide screening and rescreening, privileged access rules, separation-of-duties requirements, monitoring scope and offboarding steps. The role entries above give starting points.
  6. Treat transient states as roles. Notice periods, adverse separations, role changes and long absences change risk for anyone. Define what happens to access and monitoring in each, and make HR's notification to security automatic.
  7. Govern it. Give the register an owner, agree it with HR, legal, privacy and the business owners of the assets, and tell the workforce which roles are designated and what that means for them.
  8. Review it. At least annually, as NPSA recommends for the risk register, and sooner after a reorganization, a new critical system, a merger, a significant incident, a change in threat or a new regulation. NIST PS-2 requires position designations to be reviewed and updated at a frequency the organization defines, so write yours down.
For financial institutions. The regulators already ask for a version of this. The Federal Reserve's SR 96-37 and New York DFS's 2022 letter both expect institutions to identify sensitive positions and require time away from them. SR 96-37 says the duties should not be carried out physically or electronically during the absence; DFS asks each institution to define absence itself, covering both physical and electronic access, and allows a tiered approach. A high-risk roles register can be the same list, kept once.
